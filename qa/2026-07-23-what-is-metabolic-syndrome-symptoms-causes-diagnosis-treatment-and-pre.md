# What Is Metabolic Syndrome? Symptoms, Causes, Diagnosis, Treatment, and Prevention

Metabolic Syndrome

Overview

What Is Metabolic Syndrome?

Metabolic syndrome is usually defined as the presence of three or more of the following risk factors for chronic disease:

Hypertension (high blood pressure)

Abdominal obesity (belly fat)

Hyperglycemia (high blood sugar)

Low HDL (“healthy”) cholesterol

High triglycerides

[1]

While there is no single drug approved for metabolic syndrome, a combination of medications and new healthy eating and exercise habits can comprehensively address the root causes of the condition by promoting weight loss and improving blood sugar, blood pressure, and cholesterol.

[2]

Metabolic syndrome has occasionally gone by other names. It was originally called syndrome X, or insulin resistance syndrome. Sometimes it is abbreviated as MetS.

Signs and Symptoms of Metabolic Syndrome

Metabolic syndrome usually doesn’t have any symptoms, except in rare cases, because the components of metabolic syndrome generally have no noticeable symptoms themselves. You usually cannot, for example, feel that you have high blood pressure or high cholesterol, and people with metabolic syndrome may feel perfectly healthy. These conditions develop gradually and are generally identified by signs observed during routine testing at a doctor’s office or hospital.

One sign that you might have metabolic syndrome, however, is easy to identify on your own. Abdominal obesity, sometimes called visceral obesity, is the accumulation of fat around the midsection. The fat inside your belly, which squeezes around the vital organs, is considered much more dangerous than the jiggly fat that clings to the thighs, arms, buttocks, love handles, and elsewhere. Excess abdominal fat has a very strong correlation to cardiovascular disease and type 2 diabetes, as well as asthma, dementia, and certain types of cancer.

How Is Metabolic Syndrome Diagnosed?

Metabolic syndrome is diagnosed with simple tests that can be accomplished at a regular checkup, including a blood test to check blood sugar and lipid levels.

[17]

Waist circumference over 35 inches for women or over 40 inches for men

Fasting blood sugar over 100 mg/dL

Blood pressure above 130/85 mmHg (or use of blood pressure medication)

Triglycerides over 150 mg/dL

HDL (“good”) cholesterol below 50 mg/dL for women and 40 mg/dL for men

Different health authorities use slightly different criteria for diagnosis, but most definitions are very close to each other.

Medication Options

Today, there is no single medical treatment approved for metabolic syndrome. Instead, there is a wide range of treatment options for each individual component of the condition. By addressing each condition with targeted lifestyle changes and medications, you can treat and improve metabolic syndrome overall.

[30]

High Blood Sugar (Type 2 Diabetes) There are many medication options for type 2 diabetes, including metformin, GLP-1 agonists, SGLT-2 inhibitors, sulfonylureas, insulin, and more. These medications work in different ways to lower blood sugar levels — they can reduce the liver’s glucose production, decrease appetite, reduce the absorption of glucose during digestion, or increase natural insulin production.

High Blood Pressure (Hypertension) Medication options to treat hypertension include diuretics, beta-blockers, ACE inhibitors, calcium channel blockers, alpha-blockers, and central agonists. These drugs work in a wide variety of ways to bring blood pressure down to a healthier range.

Omega-3 fatty acids, available over the counter or with a prescription, may also help.

The Takeaway

Metabolic syndrome isn’t a single disease, it’s a combination of several distinct but related health conditions: high blood pressure, high blood sugar, unbalanced cholesterol, and excess belly fat.

People who have metabolic syndrome have significantly increased risks of type 2 diabetes and cardiovascular disease.

There is no medicine approved for the comprehensive treatment of metabolic syndrome; instead, doctors will advise lifestyle modifications and prescribe drugs to target the condition’s individual components.

With healthy lifestyle habits and support from medications, it is possible to improve every aspect of metabolic syndrome and reduce the risk of complications.
